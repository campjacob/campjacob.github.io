I don't exactly remember how I decided on pursuing my degree in social work. I had been in the Masters Commission and spent a significant amount of time volunteering at the juvenile detention center in Spokane, where I encouraged incarcerated youth to believe they could change their lives. I don't think it was a moment of inspiration, but a slow build toward understanding that this is the work I want to do in my life. The path to social work was built on that. What I do know clearly is that, when I left that program, I had decided fully on this as a career. I moved back in with my mom, started working at Jubilee Youth Ranch, and attended community college at CBC. I pretty quickly developed a fully formed plan. I'd transfer to Eastern, earn my BSW, and then get into an advanced standing program and earn my MSW. 

In a personal statement that I wrote seeking a scholarship from EWU, I described it as:

> One thing that I decided was that earning a Masters of Social Work degree was one of the best avenues to launch into this field.  It was after starting to see a vision for my life that I enrolled at the community college I am currently attending.  I said that this vision got into my veins, and by this I mean it is out of this vision that the rest of my life flows.  I want to earn my MSW so that I can be more employable and better equipped to serve my community.  Our veins carry our life’s blood, supplying all of our needs for all of our organs allowing us to survive.

I've always been proud to call myself a social worker. I also recognize, and don't want to minimize or negate, the harms we have done as a profession. Since its beginnings, social work has played two roles: helping people and regulating them for the state and the dominant culture. The regulating role has often been harmful. We have judged the poor as “undeserving,” pressured immigrants to assimilate, carried out eugenic sterilizations, policed mothers on welfare, and removed Native and other marginalized children from their families. Some of our current practices still cause harm, and I worry a lot about how involved we are in problematic systems, especially carceral systems. I am proud to be a social worker because, as a profession, we have committed to examining ourselves and improving. That self-examination is exactly what the 2022 EPAS asked of us, and what the proposed 2029 EPAS removes. 

The CSWE 2022 EPAS states that

> Social workers use rights-based, anti-racist, and anti-oppressive lenses to understand and critique the profession’s history, mission, roles, and responsibilities and recognize historical and current contexts of oppression in shaping institutions and social work. (p. 8)

My concern is not just that this language about examining our history is removed (it is, and that is a problem), but that we are moving away from this work. In 2021, the [CSWE](https://www.cswe.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/Statement_Accountability_Reconciliation_Harms_Done_Indigenous_Tribal_Peoples.pdf) publicly apologized to Indigenous and Tribal Peoples, and the [NASW](https://www.socialworkers.org/LinkClick.aspx?fileticket=29AYH9qAdXc%3d&portalid=0) publicly apologized to people of color, for the harms social work has caused.

When I was at the annual CSWE conference this year (I shared a little [bit about the experience](https://jacobrcampbell.com/blog/2026/10/back-to-back-weeks-of-travel-and-cool-projects/)), I heard so many people around me talking about and concerned with the changes to the EPAS and the removal of ADEI from our list of competencies. The following table shows the competencies from 2022 and the proposed 2029 EPAS.

| Competency | 2022 EPAS | 2029 EPAS |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Demonstrate Ethical and Professional Behavior | Demonstrate Ethical and Professional Behavior |
| 2 | **Advance Human Rights and Social, Racial, Economic, and Environmental Justice** | Promote Dignity, Respect, and Justice for All |
| 3 | **Engage Anti-Racism, Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (ADEI) in Practice** | Apply Person-in-Environment Perspective in Practice |
| 4 | Engage in Practice-Informed Research and Research-Informed Practice | Engage in Practice-Informed Research and Research-Informed Practice |
| 5 | Engage in Policy Practice | Engage in Policy Practice |
| 6 | Engage with Individuals, Families, Groups, Organizations, and Communities | Engage With Individuals, Families, Groups, Organizations, and Communities |
| 7 | Assess Individuals, Families, Groups, Organizations, and Communities | Assess Individuals, Families, Groups, Organizations, and Communities |
| 8 | Intervene With Individuals, Families, Groups, Organizations, and Communities | Intervene With Individuals, Families, Groups, Organizations, and Communities |
| 9 | Evaluate Practice With Individuals, Families, Groups, Organizations, and Communities | Evaluate Practice With Individuals, Families, Groups, Organizations, and Communities |
| 10 |  | **Ethical and Effective Use of Technology in Practice** |

While I agree that person-in-environment is a key differentiator for social workers, I am strongly opposed to the removal of Engage Anti-Racism, Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (ADEI) in Practice. The foreword of the environmental scan that informed the draft states:

> As the helping professions expand, counselors, psychologists, nurses, case managers, and technology-enabled services increasingly occupy spaces once understood as distinctly social work territory. This is not a threat to be dismissed; it is a challenge that demands a confident, well-articulated professional identity. Without one, social work risks becoming interchangeable, peripheral, or invisible at precisely the moment when its perspective is most needed. That perspective is person-in-environment—and it is irreplaceable. (p. iv)

We could and should make the same argument for ADEI. 

In the FAQ, the CSWE provide their reasoning, they explain that ADEI-related restrictions have been signed into law in 19 states, representing roughly 40 percent of accredited programs, and that their current approach of letting programs adapt the 2022 language when needed does not offer a sustainable long-term solution. Those are real pressures on real programs.

I find myself incredibly lucky, both in my institution and my state, to have a great deal of liberty and academic freedom. But what I heard from peers in states with restrictions is that our accreditation standards have been part of what protects them. Because this content is required by our accrediting body, they have been able to seek exemptions or waivers that let them keep teaching it. If the requirement disappears from the standards, that protection disappears with it, for exactly the programs these changes are meant to help.

The change is also more than a change in labels. CSWE says the underlying values and expectations have not changed, but the practice behaviors, which are what programs actually assess, tell a different story. In Competency 5, students would no longer have to advocate for policies that advance social, racial, economic, and environmental justice; they would have to "demonstrate an understanding of how to influence policy." The Competency 8 behavior of negotiating, mediating, and advocating with and on behalf of clients is gone. And the Competency 1 language asking us to critique our own history, quoted above, has been cut.

I find it discouraging that terms such as White supremacy, racism, anti-racist, oppression, and intersectionality could all be completely removed from our standards. Racism and anti-racist appear more than 30 times in the 2022 version, and oppression more than 20 times. In the proposal, none of them appear even once.

Equity, which focuses on treating people according to their circumstances so they can reach fair outcomes, appears only once in the proposal, and only about the student learning environment. Fairness, which generally focuses on treating people in a consistent way, appears often in the proposal's educational policy and in a couple of competency descriptions, but neither concept appears in any of the practice behaviors.

To its credit, CSWE has said it is listening. In an October 20 update, CSWE leadership wrote that the writing committee will reassess the language substitutions, add "stronger and more explicit language around addressing and combating racism," and strengthen the language on environmental justice. They have also said they will reassess how social work history is included. That is encouraging, and it is exactly why public comment matters.

I don't know whether it makes the most sense to take the ethical use of technology out of Competency 1's practice behaviors and make it its own competency. I think we can have a reasonable and rational argument about that, and about the pros and cons of adding a 10th competency that must be assessed. But I strongly feel that removing ADEI goes against our values as social workers. Fairness and equal opportunity aren't wrong in themselves. But if they replace an understanding of structural barriers, oppression, and the need for equity, it will be much harder for me to be proud to call myself a social worker.

If you are a social work educator, student, or practitioner, I encourage you to read the [2029 EPAS draft](https://www.cswe.org/2029-epas) and [share your feedback with CSWE](https://cswe.az1.qualtrics.com/jfe/form/SV_4TOU3VO3Ff7uBQq) before the comment period closes at 11:59 PM ET on November 20, 2026. A second draft is expected in fall 2027, with another opportunity to comment.
