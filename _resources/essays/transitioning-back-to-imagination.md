In the spring of 2002, I had just graduated from high school at a youth residential facility. The program, Jubilee Youth Ranch, was a center that worked with adolescents with problematic behaviors. My enrollment and graduation from that program changed the trajectory of my life. I have written some about my entrance into and story of going to the boarding school (Campbell, n.d.). After this experience, I went on to another intense program that would further impact my life. My experience engaging in the Master's Commission for two years is intertwined with my current career path and my enrollment in the Transformative Studies program at the California Institute of Integral Studies.

I wrote about some of the impactful experiences of being in the Master's Commission and some of the ways it has related to my current studies (Campbell, 2019). As a part of my participation in the leadership training and discipleship program, I volunteered doing manual labor and connecting with people all over the country. We did many different things, from yard work to staffing Microsoft conferences, and a lot of painting. We spent time at a teen homeless shelter painting their facility for them. It was here that I first heard about an exodus from the inner city.

After a long day of working at _New Horizons Ministries_ in Seattle and painting their facility, we gathered around with one of the center staff. He talked about some of the issues he saw facing his community and the flight of the religious community and other more affluent community members. He talked about how people are unwilling to get their hands dirty and be around the street's grittiness, especially the homeless population. I found in my heart a growing desire to be in the middle and not to shy away from the hard things.

He wove a story in which I could imagine myself and my desired commitment. Hopkins (2019) explains the importance of being a storyteller, to paint a picture that the listener can imagine themselves being involved in and participating in. As I grow older, I feel like I have lost some of the sparks of imagination. When I was in the Master's Commission, I was full of ideas and plans to change the world. I shared stories about having a communal living situation within the inner city where we focused on being involved in the community and being a supportive and safe place. This never really came to pass, and while there is still some longing for this type of work, I am still doing other good work that I'm proud to do.

As a child, one of the careers I wanted to pursue was as an inventor. I would regale adults with my theories that the speed of darkness had to be faster than the speed of light (how else could it get dark so quickly when you turned off the lights). It is not that I have lost all of my imagination, but the wide-eyed glimmer that I had before has maybe hardened some or lost some of its force. This feels exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic, but I think it is primarily related to my feeling isolated and disconnected. In a similar vein, I have some personal issues that are going on as well that could have a major impact on me right now. Being in the middle has felt like a daze and hard to focus. It feels so lonely, and the unknown can cause insecurity and fear.

I have to continually remind myself that the same unknown can also be an unknown, showing the possible. I think that is some of the magic of the idea of what if. Hopkins (2020) compiled many parts of the first nine installments of his podcast, _From What If to What Next_. During this episode, the best of 2030, there were such "what ifs" posed as birdsong drowning out the sound of traffic, universal basic income, localized food production, and so many other topics. We can take these difficult seasons in our lives or as a society and take up the mantle of fear and insecurity, or we can supplant those thoughts with imagination.

In my own life, I think there is a need to find partnership and interconnectedness. The Academy for Systems Change (n.d.), writing in the Donella Meadows Project, describes the _U Process_ as a tool for complex thinking. Also known as Theory U, it includes five stages: (1) co-initiating (stop and listen to others and what life calls you to do), (2) co-sensing (connect with people and places to sense the system from the whole), (3) presencing (go to the place of silence and allow the inner knowing to emerge), (4) co-creating (in living examples to explore the future by doing), and (5) co-evolving (that embody the new in ecosystems).

The diagram for this model shows a "U," and the process has two peaks that are about connecting with the outside world, while the very base of the illustration is about connecting inwardly. The exposure to people who are being initiated along with me is one of the great strengths of this program at CIIS. It is up to me to give myself space and silence and develop the inner portion before being ready to move forward to co-creating and co-evolving.

Developing these connections with others does more than help increase imagination. Suzanne Simard talks about the wisdom that we can get from trees. Within nature, there is a model that healthier forests are more diverse. She and Harvey (2020) talk about this need for diversity.

> If everybody's a strong competitor-- if everyone's an alpha tree, then they're going to fight each other to death, through shading and so on. If you have a structured forest where you've got small ones, and big ones helping out the small ones, and mid-canopy trees, and different species, they're occupying all the niches in that forest, that diverse forest, and so that's actually a much healthier forest, is to have that kind of diversity. (20:30)

I know that my future goals and work will be improved by becoming more diverse. The things that I hope to accomplish and the dreams that I have are more significant than I can do independently. To make them sustainable, I will need to find ways to incorporate others.
