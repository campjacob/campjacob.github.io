The concept of a world-ecology is complex. Koelsch (2012) uses a metaphor of a quilt or patchwork in her description of a qualitative methodology for understanding women who are survivors of rape. She states, "the patchwork quilt metaphor is a means to present participant data as both unique and part of a larger whole" (p. 823). This is because there are many unique examples, perspectives, and ways of understanding a given situation or problem.

There are a significant number of different ways that writers have attempted to identify complexity. Barron (1995) talks about the act of finding order out of chaos. Bateson (2010) elaborates how there ends up being a mass tangle of ideas and relationships. The concept of a patchwork might not be messy enough. Moore (2013) describes what he calls world-ecology synthesis as a dialectical understanding that we are both the subject and the object, the organism and environment, or the cause and the effect. The interrelatedness of this viewpoint seems to me to fit well into the idea of a patchwork.

The dystopian and utopian stories that we tell ourselves help paint a picture of how interrelated everything is. It became very evident in reading through Zamalin (2019) that there is a lot more I need to read from Black writers. I find myself especially drawn to his discussion of Octavia E. Butler and Sun Ra. Previously my only perspective on what might be called Black utopian ideas was that of Wakanda as laid out in the Marvel Cinematic Universe and Coogler's (2018) _Black Panther_. I found it engaging and enlightening to hear so many distinct views and ways of thinking. He identifies that "postracialism in black utopia is something else: it is a postracist condition countering the logic of racism—its power binaries, languages, ways of being and organizing collectively" (p. 141). It is these binaries that are so important.

Our world's interrelated view, especially coupled with climate change, helps add additional fire to the flame of changes needing to happen. Casey Camp-Horinek, in her keynote presentation recorded by the Bioneers (2019), says there is a call to war. She describes that the rights of nature are not separate from us. There is no separation between ecological justice and social/racial justice. In a panel discussion published by (SC)2 Video (2020) about the interrelatedness of the Green New Deal and racial justice, Kaniela Ing told a story about a telescope in Hawaii and its challenges. He described that you couldn't just talk about science as a sacred thing. It impacts other sacred things. There is an interconnectedness in everything we do.

The Green New Deal is described as a sweeping transition of the whole. It looks to have a meaningful investment in "renewable energy, energy efficiency, and clean transportation" (Klein, 2019, p. 31). It also incorporates demands for things such as free universal health care, higher education, and childcare. It is through addressing as many parts of the patchwork as possible that we can make sustainable change.
