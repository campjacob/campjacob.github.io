Slowing down and honestly sitting with a topic or idea is not something that comes easily for me. I can often go on long stretches of focusing and working, but sitting with a single topic or idea and considering all facets is something that I don't do well in one sitting. I tend to go through a little bit of it, leave it to simmer in the background, and later come back for more. Even when things are quieter and the rhythm of life is in a slower stage, I still find myself struggling to slow down. I recently came across Tricia Hersey, who runs an organization called the Nap Ministry[^1]. Her focus is on encouraging and supporting people to slow down from grind culture and take naps as a part of that process.

As a clinician, I talk about and use some mindfulness, but my implementation of mindfulness is relatively haphazard in my practice. I have also tried meditation a couple of times and find the idea of developing a regular exercise attractive, but it has never stuck. Taking naps or practicing meditation are not the only ways of slowing down. Newport (2016) makes an argument for slowing down. He describes work as falling into two categories. There is shallow work and deep work. His discussion encourages knowledge workers to find meaningful ways to move away from the distractions of the shallow work to make significant progress in our deep work through removing distractions.

It seems this process of humans participating in imagination with the external world described by Goethe is the culmination of dedicated work and practice at some of this slowing down. Lachman (2017) described:

> Hans Gebert, writing of Goethe's experience at Strasbourg Cathedral, remarked: 'Through observation, exercise, and mental effort he had penetrated to an imperceptible reality, the idea of the architect'. That is to say that through his imagination, Goethe had hit on knowledge that was otherwise inaccessible. What helped Goethe here was his 'developed inner life', what the philosopher Robert McDermott describes as 'the painstakingly trained process by which the sympathetic personal knower imaginatively gleans the ideal form of each physical object and natural process' ("The Knower and The Known," para. 18).

It was through dedication and intention to develop perceptiveness.

As Lachman (2003) describes Goethe and his seeing the _Urpflanze_, "it is only in this way, he says, when human imagination 'participates' in the external world, that truth, or at least any truth worth having, emerges" ("From Goethean Science to the Wisdom of the Human Being," para. 4). It seems this participation needs to be slow. Holdrege, sharing in Ewald (2006), describes Goethean science[^2] as being slow science. Lachman (2017) explains that his focus on the intentionality of perception is a central insight for Goethe. It seems that it is through this intense focus that he was able to find the intermediary realm between abstract, sensory, and physical.

The development of this insight seems tangentially related to having a _Eureka Moment_. I am not necessarily describing the surprise of insight, but more in the profoundness of the insight and almost seeing through something. Crowther and Schmidt (2015) describe these as states of grace and define them as occurring "in moments in analysis when there is a sudden realisation, a shared expansion of consciousness evoked by the emergence of a thought or image, which profoundly changes the quality of relating between patient and analyst" (p. 54). They are generally talking about interactions with clients and clinicians' ability to understand the client. It seems that much of this discovery is both done by slowing down and putting attention. Even when Archimedes originally is purported to have exclaimed Eureka, one imagines that he has sat with and thought about volume and measurement of irregular objects. I wonder if these moments of realization are connected with the imaginal.
