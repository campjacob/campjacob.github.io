In 2008, a friend and I traveled around Europe for about six weeks[^1]. We made our way through 10 different countries, visiting some of the largest cities and many country capitals. I had never considered myself as having a superior sense of direction before this trip (although I spent some time orienteering in Boy Scouts). It became evident that I was able to help us navigate each of the cities relatively easily and became our de facto guide. This trip took place before smartphones were everywhere with GPS and maps. I generally had an intuitive sense of how to help us get around. This sense of direction was different from that developed being in a city for a while. The ability to find our way in a metropolitan area might demonstrate some of the difference between intuition and learned discovery.

Lachman (2017) brings up Jünger's description of a midpoint and circumference in relationship to what he described as "the master key." The circumference is described as needing ant-like industriousness. This seems to relate to the challenge of knowing something through external measurement. We can learn about it by going over it, around it, and learning all of the aspects through this process. An ant-like industriousness is sometimes necessary to develop an overall internal map of a city. The midpoint is described as the gift of intuition. Instead of starting from knowing all of the aspects of something, we start in the middle. It is being able to intuit the correct direction without necessarily having to have in-depth knowledge about a place. Our sense of internal direction could perhaps be slightly improved with practice or through some skill development. Still, many other examples of this circumference and midpoints can work cooperatively together. This is very true in managing and dealing with crises.

In the classrooms I work in as a school-based social worker, we have youth who can demonstrate highly disruptive behaviors. In my experiences, this has been the most profound in elementary settings. Students in my program often have emotional and behavioral disabilities (Child with a disability or student eligible for special education, 2007). The externalizing behaviors that these students engage in are often triggered by tasks or demands placed on the student. They might act out, ranging from throwing their items on the floor and tipping desks to becoming verbally and physically aggressive. Primarily the physically aggressive behaviors and other significant incidents are described as behavioral earthquakes by Lambros et al. (1998).

In looking at how seismologists currently predict earthquakes, Lee (2013) describes that they are looking at an already started earthquake. There is an advanced P wave that is detectable that comes before the more damaging S wave. Just like these early warning signs in a natural context can give time for people to try to find ways to mitigate the damage, in my classrooms, we can try to recognize the signs of this explosive behavior and mitigate the explosiveness and impact.

There are a lot of ways this can happen. Last year, I worked with students in an out-of-school placement facilitated in a counseling center. We had a student who became upset at something in the classroom and went into the hallway. While he was there, he attempted to pull out a fire extinguisher with what appeared to be an intention of using it as a weapon. I quickly had to remove the item and get it placed in a more secure location. When a student starts to show behavior that might signal a behavioral earthquake, it is essential to look at the environment and attempt to make it safer. This might be removing items, having other students leave the setting, or moving furniture around to create more space. Recognizing this escalating behavior is an activity that we can have training and develop circumference around. It is also an area that we can have intuition. I would argue that the more of these crisis situations we experience and learn to manage, and the more secondary training and practice we receive, the more we can operate in a place where we are working to understand both the circumference and the midpoint.

Another area where intuition and finesse seem most prominent is in knowing the right thing to do in a given moment. Does the youth need some comfort and support or to have firm boundaries placed? Do I know what is starting to make them feel uncomfortable and frustrated, and can I address it before it bubbles over? There are specific training and best practices for de-escalating agitated behaviors (Nordstrom et al., 2012). These can be taught, and skills developed. There are times that we just know what the right thing is to do. Early in my career, I worked at a residential center. I had very little training. Sometimes when I would intervene with kids, I might know the right thing to do, and other times I did not know as much. There is still a significant amount of the "spirit of finesse" described by Pascal (as cited in Lachman, 2017).
