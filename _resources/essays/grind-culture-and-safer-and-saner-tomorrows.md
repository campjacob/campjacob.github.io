Tricia Hersey, being interviewed by Young (2020), provided an insightful discussion regarding grind culture. I previously had never heard of her, the Nap Ministry[^1], or considered grind culture in the context that Hersey portrayed it. She stated, "grind culture is making you not sleep, you know rest is a form of resistance because it pushes back against capitalism and white supremacy" (10:43). I found connecting current-day grind culture to having historical roots in slavery and being the ultimate in forced grind culture illuminating.

Robinson (2019) adds other terms such as toil glamour or hustle culture to this cultural phenomenon. For Robinson, resistance changes our focus from being driven by the external and moving to be drawn internally. He describes this as living from the outside in versus the inside out. Robinson explains that there is a need to be internally attuned to our inner lives. Personally and culturally, confronting grind culture is an important task and needs to be addressed from multiple vantage points. From an academic focus, Newport (2016) expresses the importance of purging as much shallow work as possible from our lives and learning to practice deep work.

In some ways, attending college and being in a Ph.D. program is very much included in a type of grind culture. There is so much to do and get done. If you don't spend the time working and doing the things, you will be passed by. In any subject, we will also come across people that know significantly more than we do. It has to be up to us to slow things down. I find that I am terrible at this slowing down. I struggle at saying no to things and feeling overly optimistic about what I can and should do. I am hoping to make progressive and minor improvements in slowing down the world.

As I move toward the next phase of my program, and away from the course work and to a sole focus on developing and implementing my research project and writing the dissertation, I hope to continue to battle with myself to slow things down. To be willing to take a nap sometimes. To not allow myself to be overcommitted and to let there be space. This slowness will improve my mental health and help me make a better final product.

Even knowing that it is better, I still find internal struggle with both slowing things down for me as an individual and the impact of slowing down culturally. I have been lucky enough to live in South America, where time is less critical, and things are slower. I struggle with letting go of the importance I grant to how much somebody does or the importance of what they do. Is it problematic if we don't have resources based on "productivity?" What would the world look like if we gave each other space and freedom to do what was in their best interest? I believe if we could, it would be in the best interest of all of us.
