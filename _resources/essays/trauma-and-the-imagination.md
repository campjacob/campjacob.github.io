> I have passed through fire and deep water, since we parted. I have forgotten much that I thought I knew, and learned again much that I had forgotten. I can see many things far off, but many things that are close at hand I cannot see.
> - Gandalf (in J.R.R. Tolkien's _The Two Towers_)

Psychological trauma and its impact is a complex issue that varies from person to person and from incident to incident. The Center for Substance Abuse Treatment (2014) explains that we can understand trauma through objective and subjective characteristics. For this short essay, we are looking at the subjective or internal sequelae of traumatic experiences. These traumatic experiences can follow from a single event or as a reaction to repeated or chronic experiencing. When there is a trauma-inducing event, the reaction sometimes happens immediately or is sometimes delayed. Some internal reactions can impact the survivor emotionally, physically, cognitively, behaviorally, and existentially. The responses vary based on the survivor's previous experiences, support system, and coping/life skills.

Everybody who experiences a trauma-producing event has a different response. For some, it might make a marked impact on their activities of daily functioning and can even result in a disability, and for others it might not have a significant effect. For people in their mid 30's and older, especially in the United States, one shared traumatic experience that most people remember was the September 11th terrorist attacks. These attacks had psychological impacts on both people living in New York (Galea et al., 2002) and around the nation (Schuster et al., 2001). Similar to many of the older generation who can tell you precisely what they were doing when they first heard about the assassination of JF Kennedy (King Cassidy, 2013), many have distinct memories and reactions to seeing the repeated news clips showing the plane flying into the Twin Towers. I was in jail at the time, but that is a story for another time[^1].

The majority of the population who experienced seeing images of these horrific attacks do not have any lasting psychological trauma. Some ten years after these attacks, I was working at a community mental health counseling center as a mental health therapist and case manager. One of my clients was a middle-aged Hispanic woman. She had a pervasive developmental disability that severely impacted her cognitive abilities. She was being treated for posttraumatic stress disorder due to her exposure to news of these terrorist attacks. She was severely affected by having had this second-hand contact with the experience. I remember being in my office with her and some of her family when a plane flew overhead. Having seen it from the window, she started cowering on the floor and tried to protect herself by taking a picture frame from my desk and holding it over her head. I remember thinking about these differences in the impact that trauma can have on each one of us at that moment.

The inner workings of our mind are a fascinating subject. Considering the imagination and the way we interact and understand the world is different for each person. Lachman (2003) uses the example of a rainbow to help describe what participatory consciousness is. He questions whether a rainbow is there or not, and if only one person sees it, could it be described as a hallucination. This rainbow, is it really there, even if it is only one person who sees it? In considering how imagination and consciousness have evolved over time, I find myself wondering about the imagination and its connection with our experience of trauma and challenges.

As Lachman (2017), in describing Barfield and his understanding of the evolution of consciousness, states:

> At an earlier time, Barfield argues, humans perceived the world in a way that we today would say was very metaphorical, very figurative, and very poetical. We no longer see it that way, or do so only when stimulated by poetry, or if we alter our consciousness through some means, usually drugs or alcohol ("Chapter 2: A Look Inside the World," para. 27)

Lachman, speaking during a recorded conference (Pari Center, 2021), shared about Colin Wilson. He described that crisis, inconvenience, and indifference can promote positive understandings and changes. It is often that people who have experienced trauma grow stronger through that phenomenon. Linley and Joseph (2004) describe adversarial growth and many of the variables that seem to impact it. Clinicians, having heard about their clients' ability to adapt, can be affected and have vicarious resilience (Engstrom et al., 2008). One thing is sure, the imagination is a powerful force.
