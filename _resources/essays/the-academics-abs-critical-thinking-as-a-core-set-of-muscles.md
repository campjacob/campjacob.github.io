Fitness professionals describe the abdomen as our core. If we are thinking about the various muscle groups in our bodies and how they relate to overall fitness and health, there are many aspects we could consider. The part that is likely the most interconnected and can impact our fitness is our stomach muscles. These core muscles, which are very aptly named, get used to varying degrees when we do all other fitness-related activities[^1].

Critical thinking could be related analogously to that of our physical core. As scholars, all things that we do should have some connection to our critical thinking skills. In exercise regimes, it is common to have days or periods focused on specific muscle groups. There might be a day for legs, cardio, core, etc. In our reading, writing, and dialog we participate in as academics, sometimes we must explicitly focus on that core. But the other days, even if critical thinking isn't directly identified and considered, there is still a need to use those core muscles.

A position of skepticism is how we should view the world, according to QualiaSoup (2009). They describe this stance as not being an indiscriminate rejection of ideas but doubting/suspending our judgment. We must slow it down and examine the claims along with their reasons, assumptions, and biases. This task is challenging and takes learning to pose ourselves in a way that is ready to be skeptical. One way to improve our ability to make poses is to understand some necessary traits for critical thinking.

Paul and Elder (2014) describe eight traits that they define as essential for developing our critical thinking skills. These include (1) intellectual humility versus intellectual arrogance, (2) intellectual courage versus intellectual cowardice, (3) intellectual empathy versus intellectual narrow-mindedness, (4) intellectual autonomy versus intellectual conformity, (5) intellectual integrity versus intellectual hypocrisy, (6) intellectual perseverance versus intellectual laziness, (7) confidence in reason versus distrust of reason and evidence, and (8) fairmindedness versus intellectual unfairness. One of these skills that I find especially salient is moving away from being narrow-minded and developing what they term intellectual empathy. As a social worker/therapist, the role of empathy is crucial (Feller & Cottone, 2003).

In my own life experience, this concept of developing intellectual empathy showcases my difficulty with critical thinking. Paul and Elder (2014) describe this as "Having a consciousness of the need to imaginatively put oneself in the place of others in order to genuinely understand them, which requires the consciousness of our egocentric tendency to identify truth with our immediate perceptions of long-standing thought or belief" (p. 14). I tend to glaze quickly over things and try to run too fast. I struggle with not slowing down enough to really understand what is being said and sit with it. On the figurative couch with my clients, I have had to cultivate a moment-by-moment connection with them. It is hard to be right there, not thinking about other things. To really be present and able to connect and try to understand what is happening to them. I will continue to need to hone this muscle in my critical thinking as well.
