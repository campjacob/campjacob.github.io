Fire is a topic that has a long history and fits within many different fields. Universities offer graduate degrees in the study of Fire Science. As a science, fire science has a long and rich history (Emmons, 1984). Evolutionarily, humans have had a long history of dependence on fire itself (Wrangham & Carmody, 2010). Literature and religious contexts provide a large amount of symbolism and connection with the topic of fire (The Fire Fighter Garage, n.d.). This short essay considers fire as a theme for change at a micro (personal) and macro-level (societal).

Ivanić et al. (2005) define biomimicry as a "discipline of applying nature's principles and it does not only changes the ways we think about designing, producing, transporting, and distributing goods and services, but also provides opportunities to deal with complex environmental and economic problems" (p. 20). Videos from Our Changing Climate (2018) and Tree TV / N2K Need to Know (2015) provide insight into biomimicry applications and examples. We can consider the topic of fire and its impact on the environment. The National Geographic Society (2020) describes that fire can help promote ecological health. They explain that periodic fires can clear out dead organic material, help plants in their life cycle, and indirectly help some animals or insects in their food web. It is the elimination of the previous parts or the waste that creates a positive ecological impact.

In the most recent episode of the podcast I am doing with my mom, we talked about [The Breaking and Breakthroughs in our Lives](https://motherdearest.show/3). We used the metaphor of fire as well to describe the process we go through. This micro process that we go through seeing changes happen in our life is significant. This imagery of fire as both a creative and destructive force is powerful. In my own life, I am currently going through a phase that could seem somewhat destructive. Change always has some element of ruination to it. As I face possible changes, I can also be looking for the new thing that will sprout out of this.

Seeing the fire and its devastation is scary. We can consider fire to be both fearsome and hopeful. The aftereffects can be the setting for a new beginning. We sometimes need fire to come in, allowing new things to start during different phases of our lives. This is also true at a macro-level. Nelson (2019) discusses the language of fire and its use by indigenous women. It is a relevant metaphor for social and societal movements. She describes:

> On the volcanic edges of creation and destruction, birth and rebirth, Indigenous women are leading and 'staying with the trouble.' With the resilience of our inherent strengths and survivance, we find ecologies of practice in many ways. Friendship, solidarity, and 'radical relationality' are critical ways to combat sorrow and grief with reverential reciprocity and belonging (p. 81).

There is a need to see how this fire can spark significant change. This need is visibly described by Klein (2019) and her discussion of the sweeping changes needed under the Green New Deal. The profession of social work also identifies other areas of social justice and transformation that need to happen within our world.

Uehara et al. (2014) help identify the grand challenges for social work in a report for the _American Academy of Social Work and Social Welfare_. These are further expanded on in the introduction to a special edition of the _Journal of the Society for Social Work and Research_ focused on these grand challenges where Uehara et al. (2017) describe these as:

- Ensure healthy development for all youth
- Close the health gap
- Stop family violence
- Advance long and productive lives
- Eradicate social isolation
- End homelessness
- Create social responses to a changing environment
- Harness technology for social good
- Promote smart decarceration
- Reduce extreme economic inequality
- Build financial capability for all
- Achieve equal opportunity and justice

Each of these areas of our society requires change. Some of the rebuilding that needs to happen can happen incrementally and through less destructive methods. I propose that most of these problems require significant systematic change and require a tearing down of the old. There is a need for a fire to come through and remove some of our society's problematic aspects.
